\section{Introduction}

\noindent\textbf{Significance of the research and prior work.}
Data is the substrate of the modern economy, and over the last decade industry analysts have repeatedly estimated that more than 80\% of it is unstructured: customer conversations, product photographs, call-center recordings, medical scans, dash-cam video, satellite imagery, regulatory PDFs. The relational stack that powers today's data warehouses, lakehouses, and BI tools was designed for the remaining 20\%. The result is a structural blind spot at the heart of every Fortune-500 analytics platform: the most strategically valuable signal in an enterprise sits in content that traditional analytics cannot read, let alone join, filter, rank, or aggregate. For two decades the workaround has been brittle, hand-coded ETL pipelines that reduce rich content to a handful of categorical features, throwing away the rest of the meaning before it ever reaches a query engine.

Large language models (LLMs) and large multi-modal models collapse this gap for the first time. A single frontier model can classify a review, score an image, transcribe and reason over an audio clip, extract structured fields from a contract, and answer free-form analytical questions, all from a natural-language specification. The database community has seized this opportunity, defining \emph{semantic operators}, declarative LLM-backed primitives such as \texttt{sem\_filter}, \texttt{sem\_join}, \texttt{sem\_map}, and \texttt{sem\_topk} that compose inside a relational plan~\cite{patel2024lotus,patel2025lotus}. LOTUS, Palimpzest~\cite{liu2024palimpzest}, DocETL~\cite{shankar2025docetl}, ThalamusDB~\cite{jo2024thalamusdb}, and emerging production engines such as Google BigQuery AI and Snowflake Cortex AISQL all embody this pattern, and a recent vision paper argues that Deep-Research-style LLM agents are themselves becoming the next generation of analytics system~\cite{russo2025deepresearch}. We are, in short, witnessing the largest shift in the analytics stack since the invention of the data warehouse: from \emph{relational analytics} to \emph{agentic analytics}, in which an autonomous agent plans the query, picks the right model for each tuple, and reasons over arbitrary modalities. The economic prize is enormous. If even a fraction of the world's unstructured content becomes routinely queryable at warehouse scale, entire industries, customer experience, healthcare, public safety, life sciences, financial compliance, will gain analytics capabilities they cannot purchase today at any price.

The catch is cost. Our recent benchmark SemBench~\cite{lao2025sembench}, built in collaboration with researchers from Cornell, MIT, Michigan, Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam, Universit{\"a}t T{\"u}bingen, Amsterdam, and Google, evaluates four state-of-the-art semantic engines (LOTUS, Palimpzest, ThalamusDB, BigQuery AI) on 55 queries spanning all four data modalities and five operator classes. The headline finding is sobering: a single semantic-join query on the 157k-row Cars scenario can incur double-digit dollar cost and tens of minutes of latency, and accuracy varies wildly between systems and models. Extrapolated to the 10M-row Amazon-reviews benchmark already used by Google~\cite{chung2026proxy}, or to the billion-row workloads that real AWS analytics customers run on Athena and Redshift, naive frontier-model pipelines cost more than the analytical insight is worth. Without a fundamentally new approach, agentic analytics will remain a research curiosity, available only to teams that can afford to burn LLM credits at the rate of a small data center. Solving this is not an incremental optimization; it is the gating problem that decides whether agentic AI ever reaches the analytics workloads where it would create the most economic value, and whether AWS or a competitor sets the platform standard for the next decade.

Four research threads address pieces of this problem but remain largely uncombined. \emph{Proxy-model query acceleration}: SUPG~\cite{kang2020supg} and TASTI~\cite{kang2022tasti} train a cheap classifier against a small oracle budget, and a recent Google paper~\cite{chung2026proxy} brings the idea into the LLM era, replacing most LLM calls in BigQuery and AlloyDB with lightweight learned proxies over embedding vectors and reporting roughly 100$\times$ cost and latency reductions for semantic filtering and ranking on a 10M-row Amazon-reviews benchmark. \emph{Model cascades and routing}: FrugalGPT~\cite{chen2023frugalgpt} and the LOTUS cascade operators~\cite{patel2025lotus} run a cheap model first and escalate only on low confidence. \emph{Cost-based optimization for semantic plans}: Abacus~\cite{russo2025abacus} searches the plan space for a semantic program under quality, cost, or latency constraints. \emph{Agentic rewriting}: DocETL~\cite{shankar2025docetl} uses an LLM agent to rewrite document-processing pipelines and improves accuracy by 21--80\%, and a recent vision paper argues Deep-Research-style agents are becoming the next analytics runtime~\cite{russo2025deepresearch}.

\noindent\textbf{How this proposal differs from and builds upon existing work.}
Each of the four threads above tackles a slice of the problem. Proxy approaches assume a fixed operator and workload; cascades reason at the call level, not at the plan level; offline cost models break under unseen LLM predicates; agentic rewriters ignore the statistical accuracy-vs-cost question. We propose to unify them under a single \emph{economic agent} that decides, per query and per tuple, how much LLM reasoning to spend and where, with statistical quality guarantees that an analyst can trust. Concretely, the project (i) lifts the sampling-and-proxy approach of~\cite{chung2026proxy} from single-operator filtering and ranking to full multi-operator plans with semantic joins and maps; (ii) couples it with a cost-based agentic optimizer that uses \emph{online} sampling, in the spirit of Abacus~\cite{russo2025abacus}, instead of offline profiling; and (iii) exposes the result as a reusable agentic skill, callable from Amazon Bedrock AgentCore, that fits inside existing AWS analytics stacks. The unexpected angle is that, when sampling, proxies, cascades, and plan-level rewriting are co-optimized, modest amounts of LLM labeling unlock plan rewrites that pure cascades or pure proxies cannot reach, e.g., predicate pushdown through a semantic join becomes safe once the agent has bounded the join's residual error.

\noindent\textbf{Open-source tools we plan to contribute to.}
SemBench~\cite{lao2025sembench} (extending the benchmark with cost-quality-aware agentic baselines and the proxy-disagreement traces produced by our agent), LOTUS (a pluggable economic-agent operator implementation), Palimpzest (an Abacus-compatible cost model that consumes our online sampling estimates), and ThalamusDB (multi-modal proxy adapters). All code will be released under a permissive license through the SemBench leaderboard and our institutional GitHub.

\noindent\textbf{AWS ML tools we will use.}
Amazon Bedrock (frontier LLMs as labelers and verifiers, with prompt caching), Amazon Bedrock AgentCore (managed deployment, memory, identity, and observability for the economic agent), Amazon Bedrock Data Automation (multi-modal feature and embedding extraction for proxy training), Amazon SageMaker AI and SageMaker Unified Studio (proxy training, hosting, and offline evaluation), Amazon Athena, Amazon Redshift, and Amazon S3 Tables/Iceberg (the data plane), and AWS Glue (ingestion and cataloging of multi-modal benchmark datasets).

\section{Methods}

\noindent\textbf{Architecture overview.}
We wrap each semantic operator and, recursively, each query plan in an \emph{economic agent}. The agent takes a natural-language operator specification, a data source, and a user budget $(\$^{\max}, T^{\max}, Q^{\min})$ on cost, latency, and quality, and returns answers together with statistical accuracy guarantees. Internally it manages three budgets in a closed loop: a \emph{labeling budget} (LLM calls spent teaching cheap proxies), a \emph{verification budget} (LLM calls spent double-checking proxy predictions), and a \emph{reasoning budget} (LLM calls reserved for tuples the proxies cannot handle). A schematic appears in the Appendix.

\noindent\textbf{Thread A: Sampling-and-proxy economic agent.}
Motivated by~\cite{chung2026proxy,kang2020supg}, the per-operator inner loop is:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.3em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{Sample} a small calibration set $S$ from the input relation using stratified or embedding-guided sampling. The sampler is itself an agent action informed by data statistics and prior queries in the session.
\item \textbf{Label} $S$ with a strong LLM (e.g., a Claude model on Amazon Bedrock) against the operator's natural-language predicate.
\item \textbf{Train} a lightweight proxy (logistic regression, gradient-boosted trees, or a compact transformer head) over existing embeddings or features extracted by Amazon Bedrock Data Automation.
\item \textbf{Deploy} the proxy on the full relation; pick a confidence threshold $\tau$ so that the residual set falls within the user's quality budget with high probability, using SUPG-style concentration bounds~\cite{kang2020supg}.
\item \textbf{Route} low-confidence tuples to the LLM, merge results, and \textbf{update} the proxy if drift is detected across micro-batches.
\end{enumerate}
The novelty over~\cite{chung2026proxy} is to (i) extend the loop from filter and rank to semantic join and map, where naive proxy training does not apply because the candidate space is quadratic; (ii) share proxies across correlated queries and scenarios within a session, exploiting the embedding cache; and (iii) expose the agent as a Bedrock AgentCore skill so any downstream analytics agent can invoke it.

\noindent\textbf{Thread B: Cost-based agentic optimizer with online sampling.}
A single-operator proxy is not enough when a query contains multiple semantic operators or when cheaper algebraic decompositions exist. Building on Abacus~\cite{russo2025abacus} and DocETL~\cite{shankar2025docetl}, we design an agentic optimizer that (a) enumerates physical plans annotated with model choice, operator implementation (exact LLM, cascade, proxy, or hybrid), and sampling strategy; (b) uses \emph{online} sampling to estimate per-operator selectivity and cost rather than offline profiling, so the optimizer adapts to previously unseen predicates and data; and (c) runs an LLM agent that proposes algebraic rewrites (filter pushdown through semantic joins, predicate reordering, map-reduce decomposition of long-context prompts) and validates them against a small held-out LLM-labeled set. We will study whether a multi-armed bandit or reinforcement-learning formulation, in which the agent allocates sampling rounds to the most uncertain plan candidates, outperforms the static cost models of Abacus on SemBench.

\noindent\textbf{Thread C: Quality guarantees, observability, and multi-modal support.}
For practicality the agent must be trustworthy. We will (i) produce per-query confidence intervals on result sets and aggregates by adapting prediction-powered inference and SUPG-style estimators to semantic joins~\cite{kang2020supg}; (ii) emit a structured execution trace, compatible with Bedrock AgentCore observability, that lets analysts see which tuples were resolved by proxies versus LLMs and why; and (iii) handle text, image, and audio modalities by routing modality-specific feature extraction through Amazon Bedrock Data Automation and Amazon SageMaker inference endpoints.

\noindent\textbf{Evaluation.}
We will evaluate on (a) the five SemBench scenarios (movie, wildlife, cars, e-commerce, MMQA), which together cover all four modalities and five operator classes; (b) the 10M-row Amazon-reviews benchmark used by~\cite{chung2026proxy}; and (c) ablations isolating the contribution of sampling, proxies, cascades, and agentic rewriting. Metrics include dollar cost, end-to-end latency, F1/precision/recall for selection operators, relative error for aggregates, and analyst acceptability from a small user study at our institution. The public target is to match the quality of a frontier-model-only pipeline while reducing dollar cost by at least $10\times$ and tail latency by at least $5\times$ across SemBench scenarios.

\section{Expected Results}

\noindent\textbf{Outcomes.} A unified agentic framework that couples sampling-based proxy acceleration with plan-level cost-based optimization for multi-modal semantic queries; the first end-to-end accuracy guarantees for semantic-join plans; pluggable economic-agent operators integrated into LOTUS, Palimpzest, and ThalamusDB; and a SemBench leaderboard track for cost-quality-latency Pareto fronts.
